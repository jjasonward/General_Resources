# Introduction

More than 35 years later, looking back, the maths that I learned at A-Level (in the UK, these are exams taken aged 16-19 pre-University) has been one of the most important foundations of my career as a scientist. I've used that level of maths most days in my career. 

These days, some mathematical capability is even more important, as data, algorithms, statistics and Artificial Intelligence (AI) are becoming central to our lives. So the investment in effort you make now studying for maths A-levels can pay large dividends across many future decades. Even with AI becoming mathematically capable, it's my belief and intuition that AI will push forward the role of maths in society, and that the skills will be needed as much as ever.

Maths, and its associated skills when developing mathematical capability, are adaptable skills that you can develop for the problem at hand, across a great many subjects and applications. 

Learning maths was also a source of joy -  getting stuck on problems and subsequently having moments of insight, in addition to developing your mind to solve problems and learning about yourself.

Here are some thoughts to help guide you.

## Mechanics vs Statistics

There's often a choice between doing mechanics or statistics modules. If you're inclined to be a physicist or engineer, mechanics makes sense - and that is the route I took because I was certainly a physicist in the making. However, if you're mixing maths with other subjects, statistics makes a lot of sense. As a discipline, statistics touches all walks of life, and a grounding in statistics is powerful in a wide variety of sectors and subjects. I wish I had got into statistics a lot sooner than I did - it empowers you to correctly interpret results from data, which feeds into decision making. I am a member of the UK's Royal Statistical Society, which is a reflection of just how important I think this part of maths is in the real world. I'll come back to this under the topic of uncertainty.

## Give yourself a chance to learn about a sub-topic before pre-judging it

Sometimes people negatively pre-judge a topic before (or as) they have started to learn it - for example "I don't like complex numbers" or "I don't like statistics". Looking at pages of maths that you haven't started learning yet (e.g. in a textbook) can look foreboding and frightening. But when you get into it and start seeing applications of the maths and have "ah-ha" moments, it comes together. So give yourself some space and time before judging.

## Practice, practice, practice

As with many disciplines, practice is the key. Do as many tests and past papers that you can get your hands on. Then, when it comes to the real exams, they will just be another set of questions and you'll have confidence. This is particularly true of maths, where there is less memorisation work than other subjects (e.g. Chemistry), so practice plays a prominent role. I didn't know this so explicitly at the time, but I had good teachers, and one teacher (who taught me mechanics) kept surprising us with tests. It worked wonders.

## Get familiar with the kinds of mistakes you make

Whilst it feels good to get improved or high marks on tests, it's the patterns of failure that are interesting, and the feedback loop you have on yourself to improve yourself. Understand the kinds of mistakes you make and tackle those, then you will improve effectively. This is true of any level you are at. Try not to be complacent if you are getting high marks - if you are making errors there is still a chance to improve and understand your weak spots.  You can keep a notebook of errors and learn about the kinds of mistakes you make, so you can spot patterns and understand the biggest issues to deal with.

## Show your working

There are a number of aspects to this. 
	1. You should make sure you show all of your working and thinking even though you are fully solving a problem and confident you have got it right. Indeed, laying out all of your working can be a key part of giving yourself the confidence you have solved a problem correctly. 
	2. Help the teachers to teach you. Showing your thinking when you are getting stuck is crucial, because this helps other people to help you. When it comes to applying to University, people will probably test you by deliberately giving you a tough problem to get you stuck and see how you cope. They are figuring out if you are teachable.
	3. Later on in professional life, you may have to have your work peer reviewed e.g. you have done an analysis on a dataset that leads to an important decision or outcome. To support transparency and build trust, you need to show how you arrived at an answer.
  
## Be curious and develop curiosity

This enters in a number of ways:
	1. Sometimes a problem is complicated and you need to explore avenues to get clarity, even though those avenues are a dead end. Curiosity is one of the drivers of exploration, and exploring in itself is part of the pleasure of the process.
	2. Maths can be studied in its own right, but it is the key language behind many subjects. This is obvious with science subjects like physics, but underpins very many subjects, and the very foundations of AI itself. It's worth taking the subject(s) you are interested in and becoming aware of how maths feeds into it.

## Draw a good diagram

A picture is a thousand words, and many people are visual thinkers. So, if the problem lends itself well to a diagram, draw the diagram.

## Visualise where you can

Trusted Youtube channels like 3Blue1Brown demonstrate just how useful it is to visualise. In my old days I only had teachers and textbooks, and if they didn't explain it in a way that worked for me, I got a bit stuck. There is much more opportunity these days to find something that works for your way of thinking, but there is wisdom needed to find good resources and avoid sloppy media. 

## Persistence

Persistence is a blessing and a curse, but mostly a blessing. Persistence is needed to solve problems when you can sense the solution exists, but the risk is that you persist down the wrong route. It's also important for learning new concepts. I often did not get things first time around (and sometimes not the second, third or fourth time). Persistence combined with looking at a problem from different perspectives invariably leads to the reward of insight.  

## Finding balance in life

It is an intense time learning a lot of maths, and it means spending a lot of life in your head. It is of no surprise that many famous historical scientists and mathematicians had other pursuits. For example, Einstein had his violin and many scientists went out for long walks for inspiration. If you have other interests that engage your mind and body in different ways, like sports, music, drama, dancing etc, these bring more balance to life, and are fundamental to ongoing positive mental health throughout life.

## Appreciating uncertainty and probability

Since I studied mechanics instead of statistics at A-level, I was a late-starter in properly understanding statistics. It wasn't until my final undergraduate year at University that I had a data intensive problem to solve that I had to work through some statistics. Human brains tend not to like uncertainty, and doing mechanics reinforced the idea that everything has a nice predictable outcome.  Then my mental world got challenged when I had to (a) analyse data and (b) deal with quantum physics, which is inherently uncertain. So the sooner you feel comfortable that statistics as a branch of maths deals with uncertainty, probability and risk, the better. For me, it lead, in time, to a much deeper acceptance of uncertainties in life. I even wrote a blog about it in 2019, see  https://www.valtech.com/blog/know-thy-error/

## Stay calm and breathe

There was a thing I didn't consciously recognise until my mid-twenties, which is the role of quietening the mind in relation to problem solving. This is related to the prior point on finding balance in life and how people seek inspiration. It is paradoxical, because it's tempting to think that technical problem solving is in the realm of conscious thought. However, once the brain knows what the problem is, in detail, it's beneficial to quieten the brain down whilst the problem is still in focus. This can be done by paying attention to the breathing, which also helps to calm you down. It's also why other activities like exercise and music are helpful. After some moments or minutes of quietness, I'd often find an answer would pop into mind, sometimes a surprising one. Intelligence is more than just a string of words in your conscious thought. Experiment with it! 

## Book Recommendations

1. "A Mind for Numbers: How to Excel at Math and Science (Even If You Flunked Algebra)" by Barbara Oakley PhD. An entire book, in the same vein as my thoughts above. A must read if it's the one thing you get from this book list.
2. "How to Solve It: A New Aspect of Mathematical Method" by George Polya. 
3. (Two books) "The Art of Statistics: Learning from Data" and "The Art of Uncertainty: How to Navigate Chance, Ignorance, Risk and Luck", both  by David Spiegelhalter. Given that Data and AI are so central to our lives, having an appreciation of uncertainty and risk as soon as possible is a big benefit.
